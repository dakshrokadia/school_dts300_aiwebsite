What is AI?

The short version: software that learns patterns from examples instead of being told the rules.

Starting with the name

Artificial intelligence is a label for computer systems that do things we would normally call thinking: recognising a face, understanding a sentence, spotting something odd in a medical scan. It is a broad term, and that is part of why it gets confusing. A spam filter and a self-driving car are both described as AI, and they have very little in common.

The word "intelligence" also does more work than it should. These systems are not thinking in the way a person does. They are finding patterns in very large amounts of information, then using those patterns to make a guess about something new.

How it differs from ordinary software

Normal software follows rules a person wrote. If you build a program to spot spam email, you might write rules like "if the subject line is in capitals and mentions a prize, mark it as spam". A human decides every rule in advance.

AI works the other way round. Instead of writing the rules, you show the system thousands of emails that people have already sorted into spam and not spam. It works out for itself which patterns tend to separate the two. Nobody writes down "capital letters are suspicious". The system notices it.

That difference matters for a practical reason. Because nobody wrote the rules, nobody can always explain exactly why the system made a particular decision. This comes up again on the ethics page, because it makes some AI decisions hard to challenge.

What "training" means

Training is the process of showing a system examples until it gets good at the task. The examples are called training data, and the result is called a model: the thing that has learned the patterns and can now be used on new information.

An easy way to picture it: if you looked at ten thousand photos labelled "cat" or "not a cat", you would eventually get a feel for what makes a cat a cat, without ever writing down a definition. Training is that, done by a computer, at a much larger scale.

This is also why training data matters so much. A system that only ever saw photos of ginger cats will do badly on a black one. It did not learn what a cat is. It learned what the examples it was given looked like.

Words you will hear

  • Machine learning is the general name for software that learns from examples rather than following written rules. Most of what people call AI today is machine learning.
  • Neural network is one method for doing it, loosely inspired by how brain cells connect. The comparison is looser than it sounds, and you do not need to understand brains to understand what it does.
  • Large language model, often shortened to LLM, is a system trained on enormous amounts of text so it can predict what words come next. Chatbots are built on these.
  • Algorithm just means a set of steps for doing something. It is much older and much more boring than the news makes it sound. A recipe is an algorithm.

All of these are defined again on the glossary page.

What it cannot do

AI systems do not understand what they are saying or seeing. A language model that writes a convincing paragraph about New Zealand history has no idea what New Zealand is. It has learned which words tend to follow other words.

That leads to a specific and well known problem: these systems will state something false with exactly the same confidence as something true, because confidence is not something they measure. They produce the most likely looking answer, and the most likely looking answer is sometimes wrong.

They also cannot do anything genuinely outside their training. A model trained on English text will not suddenly work in Te Reo. A model trained on photos from 2015 knows nothing about 2026.
