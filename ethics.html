AI Ethics

The arguments people are actually having, and why there are no easy answers.

Bias

An AI system learns from the examples it is given, so it also learns whatever unfairness is already in those examples. This is not the system deciding to be unfair. It is the system copying a pattern it was shown.

The well known case is Amazon, which built a tool in 2014 to sort job applications. It was trained on ten years of CVs the company had received, most of them from men. It learned that men were the pattern, and started marking down CVs that mentioned women's sports teams or women's colleges. Amazon scrapped it.

The hard part is that nobody wrote a rule saying "prefer men". It came out of the training data, which means you cannot fix it by reading the code. You have to notice it in the results.

Privacy

These systems need enormous amounts of data, and that data comes from somewhere. Photos posted online, medical records, what you searched for last week. Often it was collected for one reason and later used for another.

There is a real argument on both sides here. The same health data that feels invasive to hand over is what makes a cancer screening tool work. The question is usually less "should this data exist" and more "who agreed to this, and did they understand what they were agreeing to".

Jobs

AI can now do parts of jobs that used to need a person: writing first drafts, sorting applications, answering routine customer questions, checking documents.

Predictions here have a poor track record, in both directions. People said ATMs would end bank jobs, and the number of bank tellers went up for decades afterwards. But it would be equally wrong to say nothing changes. Some work genuinely disappears, and the people who lose it are rarely the people who get the new jobs that appear.

Misinformation

AI can now produce convincing fake photos, video and audio cheaply and quickly. It can also produce large volumes of plausible text, which makes it useful for anyone wanting to flood a topic with nonsense.

There is a second, quieter problem. As people learn that fakes are easy, real evidence gets easier to dismiss. Someone caught on camera can simply claim the footage was generated. The damage is not only that fakes are believed, but that real things stop being.

Nobody can explain the decision

Because these systems find their own patterns rather than following written rules, it is often impossible to say exactly why a particular decision was made. That is uncomfortable when the decision is whether you get a loan, a job interview, or bail.

If a human turns you down, you can ask why and challenge the answer. If a model turns you down, there may be no answer to give.

Why this is hard

None of these have a clean solution, and most of them are trade-offs rather than problems to be fixed. More data makes systems more accurate and less private. Stricter rules reduce harm and slow down useful things like medical research.

Being able to describe the trade-off honestly is more useful than picking a side, which is why this page tries to give both.
